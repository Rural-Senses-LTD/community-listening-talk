# Script: AI Will Make Community Listening Easier. Will We Actually Listen?

Yau Levy, Rural Senses. 3 minutes, 446 words (about 150 words a minute). The last sentence on each slide leads into the next one.

## 1. Title (0:00 - 0:11)
In 2020, I co-authored one of the first peer-reviewed papers applying AI to sustainable development. The technology worked. What surprised me was what happened next.

## 2. The 2020 paper (0:11 - 0:34)
With researchers from Cambridge and Oxford, we showed that audio clips and field notes could identify community needs faster and more accurately than traditional assessments. Back then, findings often arrived months after the decisions were made. We automated everything after the interview: transcription, translation, analysis and reporting. Months became weeks, sometimes days. So we expected organisations to line up.

## 3. The quote (0:34 - 1:02)
Some did. Efficiency for Access, CLASP, Days for Girls and Fondation Botnar were early adopters. Many others were not interested. Off the record, I heard this.
*(Pause.)* "It doesn't matter what the community say. The project plan was already funded and we can't change it." *(Pause.)*
By removing the technical barriers, AI removed our excuses. What was left were our human and institutional flaws. Six years later, the technical barriers are even lower.

## 4. Listening is now almost free (1:02 - 1:16)
Feedback from a group like this one can now be transcribed, translated and analysed in minutes, and more people can answer in their own language. Listening is almost free. Now look at what has not changed.

## 5. The chart (1:16 - 1:27)
Getting from interview to insight went from months to minutes. Getting from insight to a changed decision still takes a grant cycle. That gap was never about technology.

## 6. The hard questions (1:27 - 1:48)
What remains are incentives, funding restrictions, power and risk tolerance. If a community says we are solving the wrong problem, can the grantee change direction? If evidence contradicts the theory of change, is adapting a sign of learning or of failure? Until we answer these questions, faster listening brings risks of its own.

## 7. Three traps (1:48 - 2:16)
I see three traps. First, tools without power: communities give more data, headquarters get better dashboards, and power stays where it was. Second, listening without changing: once feedback arrives every week, the gap between what communities say and what we do becomes impossible to ignore. Third, trust: the same AI that makes listening easier makes deception easier, from synthetic audio to fake community feedback. Some organisations escaped all three.

## 8. Three moves (2:16 - 2:39)
Over six years I have worked on AI projects with more than 100 funders and implementers. The ones that escaped these traps changed how they were structured. They paired tools with authority, settling up front which decisions local teams would own. They made adaptation the default. And they invested in trust, treating long-term relationships as core infrastructure.

## 9. AI is a mirror (2:39 - 3:00)
Which brings me back to my question. Will we actually listen? AI is a mirror. It shows whether what communities tell us changes what we decide. Mirrors are uncomfortable, but useful if we are willing to look. So let's measure success by how often it does. Thank you.
