AI will make community listening easier.

Will we actually listen?

Yau Levy - Co-founder, Rural Senses

yau@ruralsenses.comruralsenses.com

In 2020, I co-authored one of the first peer-reviewed papers applying AI to sustainable development. What happened next surprised me, and it is why I want to ask one question today.

In 2020, we cut the wait for community insight from months to weeks

The method we published at EMNLP with Cambridge and Oxford automated everything after the interview, sometimes cutting the wait to days.

April 2020

We worked with researchers from the Universities of Cambridge and Oxford to show that audio clips and field notes could identify community needs faster and more accurately than traditional assessments. Reaching people was hard, translation was slow, and qualitative analysis needed specialist teams, so findings often arrived months after the decisions were made. We automated transcription, translation, analysis and reporting. Months of work became weeks, sometimes days.

What I heard off the record

“

It doesn't matter what the community say. The project plan was already funded and we can't change it.

By removing the technical barriers, AI removed our excuses.

We assumed organisations would be eager to hear what communities wanted. That was a bit naive. Some great organisations were, like Efficiency for Access, CLASP, Days for Girls and Fondation Botnar. But off the record, I heard this. [Pause, read the quote.] By removing the technical barriers, AI removed our excuses. What was left were our human and institutional flaws.

Listening is now almost free

Large volumes of feedback now take minutes to process, and more people can answer in their own language.

Today the technology is far ahead of what we published. Speech recognition, translation and summarisation have improved dramatically. People can answer in their own language, and large volumes of feedback can be processed in minutes. Even the interview itself can sometimes be run by an AI voice call or a WhatsApp bot.

Listening got fast. Changing course did not.

What remains are incentives, funding restrictions, power and risk tolerance. If a community says we are solving the wrong problem, can the grantee change direction? If evidence contradicts the theory of change, is adaptation learning or failure? These questions are now the core of the job.

3 traps hidden inside AI efficiency

AI efficiency can leave organisations worse off

Tools without power

Communities provide more data, headquarters produce better dashboards, and power stays where it was.

Listening without changing

Once feedback arrives every week, the gap between what communities say and what organisations do is plain to see.

The trust trap

Synthetic audio, fake video and AI-generated "community feedback" become cheaper and more convincing.

I see three traps. First, tools without power: communities give more data, headquarters get better dashboards, and power stays where it was. Second, listening without changing: grants lock in activities and budgets, and once feedback arrives every week, the gap between what communities say and what we do becomes impossible to ignore. Third, trust: the same AI that makes listening easier makes deception easier, from synthetic audio to fake community feedback.

3 moves for organisations that want to listen

The organisations that escaped these traps changed how they were structured

Pair tools with authority

When local teams get AI tools, settle which decisions they will own: priorities, resources, programme design.

Make adaptation the default

Flexible resources and governance that can approve a change within days. A change backed by evidence is good management.

Invest in trust

Treat long-term relationships as core infrastructure and the sector’s first defence against misinformation.

Over six years I have worked on AI projects with more than 100 funders and implementers. The organisations that escaped these traps made three moves. They paired tools with authority, settling up front which decisions local teams would own. They made adaptation the default, so a change backed by evidence counts as good management. And they invested in trust, treating long-term relationships as core infrastructure.
